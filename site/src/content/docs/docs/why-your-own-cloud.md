Most of us handed our digital lives to a handful of companies without ever really deciding to. It happened one convenient step at a time: photos to Google, files to Dropbox, notes to Apple, passwords to a browser. Each step was free, easy, and reversible — until, collectively, they weren't.

## The ownership boundary

Where your data lives decides who can touch it — and on what terms. It helps to compare three arrangements:

**Big Tech's cloud.** "Free" services cost nothing because the contents are the business: what you store, write, and search feeds advertising profiles and, increasingly, AI training. That model serves its real stakeholders — advertisers and shareholders — and you, the consumer, are not on the list. But the deeper trouble starts after you've settled in: the terms are theirs to change, and your switching cost is their leverage. The pattern keeps repeating across the industry — software you bought becomes a subscription, the ecosystem quietly closes around your files, and one day the fine print announces that the work you stored in the company's cloud may be used to train its AI. And it isn't only the free tiers: paying customers have watched the same clauses appear in products they'd trusted for decades. As long as someone else owns the software and the terms, both can be rewritten under you.

**A rented private cloud server is like a bank's safe-deposit box.** You pay for space and discretion, and paying changes everything: the provider's product *is* your privacy, not your data. Could the bank drill open your box? Technically yes — and a hosting provider can technically read a server's disk, and will cooperate when law enforcement shows up with a warrant. But that is essentially the only case, because every incentive points the other way: there is nothing to gain from peeking and a reputation — the thing the whole business stands on — to lose. It is a relationship built on paid trust, and it works the same way for a cloud server as it does for a vault.

**Your own hardware is a safe in your own house.** No third party in the arrangement at all. The disk is in your home, under your roof, your jurisdiction, and your physical control — the strongest form of ownership there is.

My Own Suite runs on the last two, and both restore the boundary that matters most: the software touching your files is open source — inspectable by anyone, changeable by you. There is no terms-of-service between you and your own files, no clause that can quietly change, and if a company behind an app disappears tomorrow, your installation keeps working. Choosing between a rented server and a machine at home is a practical decision, not a moral one; [How to get started](/docs/getting-started/) walks through it.

## The honest trade

Self-hosting is not magic, and these docs won't pretend otherwise. When you run your own cloud, *you* are the operator: you keep backups, you install updates, you own the failure modes. Historically that meant being a Linux administrator.

My Own Suite exists to shrink that trade to something anyone can accept:

- Installation is a guided flow, not a weekend of terminal sessions.
- Backups are manual whole-suite bundles written to an attached drive at home or a mounted block-storage volume on a cloud server.
- Updates are managed from the browser.
- The apps are chosen because they are mature, actively maintained, and loved by their communities — not because they are fashionable.

You take on a little responsibility. In exchange, nobody can read your files, mine your photos, raise your rent, or lock you out of your own life.

## You don't have to move everything at once

The most successful exits from Big Tech are gradual. Start with one thing — photo backup, or file sync, or passwords — and live with it for a month. When it feels boring and reliable, move the next thing. My Own Suite is deliberately built for that pace: every app is optional, and each one you add works with the rest.
